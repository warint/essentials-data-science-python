Session 1 ended by fixing a curve with a log. This session is about the cases a single transformation cannot reach: a genuine optimum, an effect that depends on another variable, and a group difference you have not measured.

2 · Load the data, and look at it

import essentials as ess

d = ess.load("sourdough")
d.shape
d.head()

240 loaves. Eight bakers, thirty loaves each. For every loaf: the hydration of the dough, the oven temperature, how old the starter was, the flour, and how far the loaf rose.

Answer these in Python before class:

  1. What is the mean rise, and the mean rise by baker?
  2. Plot rise_mm against hydration_pct. What shape is it?
  3. Now plot the same thing coloured by baker. What changed?
  4. What is the mean hydration_pct by baker? Are the bakers working in the same part of the design?

Why question 4 matters

If each baker works at a different hydration, then “hydration” and “which baker” are partly the same variable. Any effect you attribute to one could belong to the other, and the regression has no way to tell them apart unless you let it.

That is the trap in this session’s data, and it costs about four percentage points of hydration in the final answer. Come to class knowing the bakers are not interchangeable.

3 · Three shapes a line cannot make

A hill

Rise increases with hydration, then decreases. There is a best value, and a straight line will tell you the best value is at whichever edge of the data it is sloping towards.

The fix: a squared term. \(y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \beta_2 x^2\), with \(\beta_2 < 0\) for a hill. The peak sits at \(-\beta_1 / 2\beta_2\).

Three shapes a line cannot make (cont.)

A slope that depends on something else

The best oven temperature might be different for wet dough than for dry dough. That is not two separate effects — it is one effect conditional on the other.

The fix: an interaction. hydration_pct:oven_c in a formula, or hydration_pct*oven_c for the two main effects and the interaction together.

A level that differs by group

One baker’s loaves rise 9 mm more than another’s, for reasons no column records: their oven, their hands, their kitchen.

The fix: fixed effects. C(baker) adds one dummy per baker and one intercept each, and the other coefficients are then estimated within bakers.

5 · Self-check

On paper.

  1. A regression gives rise ~ hydration a coefficient of +0.8. Someone concludes “wetter dough always rises more”. What would you need to check before believing that?
  2. In y ~ x + z + x:z, what does the coefficient on x now mean? Be precise — it is no longer “the effect of x”.
  3. You add C(baker) to a model and the hydration coefficient changes a lot. What have you learnt?

Question 2 is the one people get wrong for years. With an interaction present, the coefficient on x is the effect of x when z is zero — which may not be a value z can take.

6 · What the session will ask of you

  • Fit and interpret a quadratic, and locate its optimum from the coefficients
  • Fit and interpret an interaction, and say what the main effects now mean
  • Add fixed effects, and explain what changed and why
  • Compare non-nested models on AIC, and say why that is not model selection
  • Recognise when a group variable is confounded with a variable of interest
