The Sourdough Optimum

Hand in a recipe. It has to be the right one.

The brief

240 loaves from eight bakers. For each: the hydration of the dough, the oven temperature, the age of the starter, the flour, and how far it rose.

Deliverable: a recipe. One hydration percentage and one oven temperature, with an honest statement of how precisely you know them.

There is a planted optimum. It is an interior point, not an edge, and it can only be recovered by a model that has all three of the right pieces in it. Get one piece wrong and you will hand in a confident recommendation that is several points out.

Stage 1 · The naive recommendation — 10 min

import essentials as ess
import statsmodels.formula.api as smf

d = ess.load("sourdough")
flat = smf.ols("rise_mm ~ hydration_pct + oven_c", data=d).fit()
  1. What recipe does this model recommend? Maximise its prediction over the range of the data and see where it puts you.
  2. Where did the answer land, relative to the design?
  3. Say in one sentence why a linear model can never do anything else.

Stage 2 · Find the hill — 20 min

  1. Add I(hydration_pct**2). Is the coefficient negative?
  2. Compute the peak from the coefficients: \(x^\star = -\beta_1 / 2\beta_2\).
  3. Now do the same for oven temperature.
  4. Report what one extra point of hydration is worth at 65%, at 80%, and at 92%. The three numbers are not the same. Explain why.

Question 4 is the one that shows whether you understand what you fitted. Once a squared term is in the model, there is no such thing as “the effect of hydration” — only its effect at a value.

Stage 3 · Find the tilt — 20 min

  1. Add hydration_pct:oven_c. Report the coefficient and its \(t\).
  2. Using the coefficients, compute the best oven temperature at 65% hydration and at 90% hydration. How far apart are they?
  3. State, in one sentence, what an additive model gets wrong about this dough.

Hint for 2: differentiate with respect to oven_c and set to zero. The hydration term does not disappear — that is the whole point.

Stage 4 · Find the bakers — 20 min

  1. Print the mean hydration_pct and mean rise_mm by baker. Look at the two columns together before you fit anything.
  2. What do you notice? State the confounding in one sentence.
  3. Add C(baker). Report the optimum before and after.
  4. Report the \(R^2\) of the model without baker effects.

Step 4 is the point of the whole session. A model can explain ninety per cent of the variance, have every coefficient significant, and give advice four percentage points wrong — with nothing in its own output to warn you.

Stage 5 · How sure are you? — 10 min

  1. Use .get_prediction(...).summary_frame() to put a confidence band on the predicted rise across a range of hydration, holding temperature at your estimated optimum.
  2. Over what range of hydration is the predicted rise indistinguishable from its maximum?
  3. Rewrite your recommendation as a range.

A peak estimated from data is flat near the top. “86.7%” implies a precision you do not have; “somewhere between 82 and 91%” is what you actually know.

Also worth trying, if you have time

  • Add starter_age_days and flour. What happens to \(R^2\)? To adjusted \(R^2\)? To AIC? What does that tell you about the two variables?
  • Compare your models on AIC. Then say why the model you are reporting was chosen by you, and not by the data.
  • Fit hydration_pct*oven_c instead of hydration_pct:oven_c and compare. Which one did you actually want, and why?

What you hand in

250 words, plus your script:

  • The recipe: hydration and temperature, as a range
  • The interaction coefficient, and the two conditional temperatures it implies
  • The optimum with and without baker fixed effects, and the \(R^2\) of the model without them
  • One sentence on what fixed effects cost you here
  • One thing this analysis does not license you to claim

And the standing rule: one output you identified as wrong, and how.

What loses marks

  • A recommendation at the edge of the design, handed in without comment
  • Interpreting a main effect as “the effect of” when a squared term or an interaction is in the model
  • Reporting an optimum to two decimal places with no interval
  • Adding C(baker) because the slides did, with no statement of what changed
  • Choosing a model on AIC and reporting it as though the data had chosen it

Two-minute report

One recipe as a range, with the temperature it is conditional on
One confounder the baker problem, in one sentence
One cost what the fixed effects took away from you
