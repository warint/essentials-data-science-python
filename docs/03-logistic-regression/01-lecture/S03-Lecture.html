What session 3 is for

The mathematics

  • Model the logit, not the probability
  • \(e^{\beta}\) is an odds ratio, not a probability ratio
  • The likelihood-ratio test: \(2(\ell_1 - \ell_0) \sim \chi^2_{\Delta k}\)
  • Expected loss is minimised at \(t^\star = c_{FP}/(c_{FP} + c_{FN})\)

The Python

  • smf.logit and .predict — which returns probabilities, not classes
  • np.exp on a coefficient to get an odds ratio
  • confusion_matrix and classification_report
  • roc_curve, and choosing a point on it

The judgement

  • Refuse accuracy as a headline number on imbalanced data
  • Write the two costs down before choosing a threshold
  • Say which error you chose to make more of, and why
  • Report an odds ratio without implying it is a probability

What we fit, and why

A linear model on a 0/1 outcome produces predicted probabilities above 1 and below 0. So model the log-odds, which are free to be any real number:

\[ \log\frac{p}{1-p} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \cdots + \beta_k x_k \]

and invert to recover a probability:

\[ p = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-\eta}}, \qquad \eta = \beta_0 + \textstyle\sum_j \beta_j x_j \]

There is no closed-form solution. The coefficients are found by maximum likelihood — numerically, by iteration — which is why the output reports a log-likelihood rather than an \(R^2\).

One feature is doing most of the work

volva_present   coefficient +4.292   odds ratio x73

share poisonous, with a volva:     61.1%
share poisonous, without one:      21.6%

A volva is the membranous sac at the base of the stem that marks the genus responsible for most fatal mushroom poisonings. The model found it without being told, and it multiplies the odds by about seventy.

It multiplies the odds, not the probability. A mushroom already at \(p = 0.95\) cannot become 70 times more likely to be poisonous. Odds ratios are constant across the range; probability changes are not.

The same coefficient, in probability terms

for base_p in (0.01, 0.10, 0.50, 0.90):
    base_odds = base_p / (1 - base_p)
    new_odds = base_odds * np.exp(b)
    new_p = new_odds / (1 + new_odds)
    print(f"p = {base_p:.2f}  ->  {new_p:.4f}   "
          f"(a rise of {new_p - base_p:+.4f})")
p = 0.01  ->  0.4249   (a rise of +0.4149)
p = 0.10  ->  0.8904   (a rise of +0.7904)
p = 0.50  ->  0.9865   (a rise of +0.4865)
p = 0.90  ->  0.9985   (a rise of +0.0985)

Same odds ratio, four different probability changes. This is why “the effect” of a logistic coefficient is not a single number, and why reporting marginal effects at chosen values is often clearer than reporting the coefficient.

Inference

Two tests, and when to use each

One coefficient: the Wald test

The \(z\)-statistic in the output is \(\hat\beta / \mathrm{se}(\hat\beta)\), compared against a normal rather than a \(t\) distribution — maximum likelihood gives asymptotic standard errors.

Several at once: the likelihood-ratio test

To test whether a group of variables belongs — all three habitat levels, say — compare log-likelihoods:

\[ 2\left(\ell_{\text{full}} - \ell_{\text{restricted}}\right) \sim \chi^2_{\Delta k} \]

from scipy import stats

reduced = smf.logit(
    F.replace(" + C(habitat, Treatment('grassland'))", ""), data=d).fit(disp=0)

lr = 2 * (fit.llf - reduced.llf)
df = int(fit.df_model - reduced.df_model)
print(f"LR statistic {lr:.2f} on {df} df,  p = {stats.chi2.sf(lr, df):.2e}")
print(f"pseudo R2 {reduced.prsquared:.4f} -> {fit.prsquared:.4f}")
LR statistic 21.27 on 2 df,  p = 2.40e-05
pseudo R2 0.5204 -> 0.5331

McFadden’s pseudo-\(R^2\) is not a share of variance and is not comparable to a linear model’s \(R^2\). Values of 0.2 to 0.4 are considered excellent. Do not report it as though it were \(R^2\).

Turning a probability into a decision requires a threshold, and the model has no opinion about what it should be. Choosing 0.5 is a choice, not a default — and on this data it is the wrong one.

The baseline you have to beat

                           poisonous mushrooms eaten: 402 of 402

Seventy-one per cent accurate, from a rule that requires no data, no model and no thought — and that will eventually kill you. Any accuracy figure you report has to be read against this number.

The threshold is a decision

Write the costs down first

The two errors are not the same size

False negative you call it edible, it is poisonous. A hospital visit.
False positive you call it poisonous, it was edible. A discarded mushroom.

Say a hospital visit is 50 times worse than losing a mushroom. Then expected loss is \(c_{FN} \times FN + c_{FP} \times FP\), and it is minimised at

\[ t^\star = \frac{c_{FP}}{c_{FP} + c_{FN}} = \frac{1}{51} = 0.0196 \]

Accuracy is the special case where the two costs are equal. It is a strange assumption, and it is almost never stated out loud.

The loss curve

theory       t = 0.0196   loss 630
empirical    t = 0.0305   loss 614
within 5% of the minimum:  t in [0.0210, 0.0305]

The two do not coincide exactly, and they should not: with 1,400 discrete cases the loss is a step function, so its minimum is a flat region rather than a point. Theory names 0.0196; the empirical floor runs from about 0.021 to 0.031, and the loss at the theoretical value is within 3% of the best achievable.

The formula is a guide to the right neighbourhood, not a value to quote to four decimal places. What matters is that it puts you two orders of magnitude away from 0.5.

Read the two rows that matter

t = 0.5000   accuracy 0.877   missed 105   discarded 26%   loss 5317
t = 0.0196   accuracy 0.585   missed   1   discarded 70%   loss 630

The cost-optimal rule has worse accuracy — 0.585 against 0.877 — and cuts expected loss by a factor of eight. It discards 70% of the basket and misses one poisonous mushroom in four hundred.

That is the session in two lines. The model that scores worse on the conventional metric is the one you would actually carry into the woods.

The ROC curve is the whole menu

AUC summarises the curve over every threshold, which is why it is a good measure of the model and a bad basis for a decision. You do not deploy a curve; you deploy a point on it.

Ordinal outcomes

When the categories are ordered

Edible, upset stomach, hospital, fatal

Those four are ordered, and collapsing them to a binary throws that away. An ordinal (proportional-odds) model uses one set of coefficients and a set of cut-points:

\[ \log\frac{\Pr(Y \le j)}{\Pr(Y > j)} = \alpha_j - \textstyle\sum_k \beta_k x_k \]

from statsmodels.miscmodels.ordinal_model import OrderedModel

sub = d.dropna(subset=["toxicity"]).copy()
X = pd.get_dummies(
    sub[["odor_index", "volva_present", "ring_present", "spore_print"]],
    columns=["spore_print"], drop_first=True, dtype=float)

ord_fit = OrderedModel(sub.toxicity.cat.codes, X, distr="logit").fit(
    method="bfgs", disp=0)
print(ord_fit.params.round(4).to_string())
odor_index           0.5314
volva_present        3.7000
ring_present         1.7350

spore_print_pink     3.1286

What that model assumes

One coefficient per variable, shared across all the thresholds. The effect of a volva on the odds of “at least upset stomach” is assumed identical to its effect on the odds of “fatal”. That is the proportional-odds assumption, and it is what buys you the parsimony.

If it is false — if a feature predicts mild illness but not death — the model will average the two and report something true of neither. Check it by fitting separate binary logits at each cut-point and comparing the coefficients. If they diverge, use a multinomial model and give up the ordering.

What to report

  • Coefficients as odds ratios, with intervals
  • The confusion matrix, not accuracy alone — and always against the majority-class baseline
  • The threshold you chose and the costs that justify it
  • Which error you deliberately made more of
  • AUC for the model; a chosen point on the ROC curve for the decision

A classifier reported without its threshold, or a threshold reported without its costs, is an unfinished piece of work.

Key takeaways

  1. Model the log-odds; recover probabilities by inverting.
  2. \(e^{\beta}\) is an odds ratio. It is constant; the probability change it implies is not.
  3. Accuracy is a trap on imbalanced data. Always quote the majority-class baseline beside it.
  4. The threshold is a decision that belongs to the costs, not to the model. \(t^\star = c_{FP}/(c_{FP} + c_{FN})\).
  5. The best decision rule can have the worse accuracy. If that sounds wrong, the metric is wrong.
  6. Ordinal models buy parsimony with the proportional-odds assumption. Check it.
