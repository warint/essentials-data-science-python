2 · The shape of the problem

Every model so far had many more rows than columns. This session starts with the opposite: 60 rows, 200 columns.

When \(p > n\), the matrix \(X^\top X\) is singular. There is no unique least-squares solution — there are infinitely many, and every one of them fits the training data perfectly, with zero residuals.

This is not a numerical inconvenience. A model that fits perfectly has learnt the noise, and it will predict new data badly. The way out is to stop asking for the best fit, and deliberately accept a worse one.

3 · Bias and variance

Take the expected squared prediction error at a point and decompose it:

\[ \mathbb{E}\left[(y - \hat f(x))^2\right] = \underbrace{\text{Bias}^2}_{\text{wrong on average}} + \underbrace{\text{Variance}}_{\text{unstable}} + \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{irreducible}} \]

Bias how far the average fit is from the truth. High for models too simple to represent it.
Variance how much the fit moves if you resample the data. High for flexible models.
\(\sigma^2\) noise. No method removes it, and no method should appear to.

The trade you are about to make

Least squares is unbiased. That sounds like a virtue, and with \(p\) close to \(n\) it is a disaster: unbiasedness is bought with enormous variance, and total error is what you care about.

Every method in this session adds bias on purpose to buy a larger reduction in variance. A deliberately wrong estimator that predicts better is not a paradox; it is the whole design.

4 · Cross-validation

You cannot choose how much to shrink using the data you fitted on: more flexibility always fits better. So hold data back.

\(k\)-fold cross-validation. Split the rows into \(k\) parts. For each part: fit on the other \(k-1\), predict the held-out part, record the error. Average.

from sklearn.model_selection import KFold, cross_val_score

kf = KFold(10, shuffle=True, random_state=0)
cross_val_score(model, X, y, cv=kf, scoring="neg_mean_squared_error")

shuffle=True matters. Without it, KFold takes contiguous blocks, and if your rows are sorted by anything at all the folds are not exchangeable.

What cross-validation is optimising

It estimates prediction error. That is worth saying twice, because this session’s puzzle turns on it: the penalty that predicts best is not the penalty that identifies the right variables. Cross-validation will hand you a model with too many variables in it, and it will be right to.

7 · Self-check

  1. A model has zero training error. What do you know about its test error?
  2. Two estimators: A is unbiased with variance 100; B has bias 2 and variance 10. Which has lower expected squared error?
  3. You cross-validate to pick a penalty, then report the selected model’s \(p\)-values. What is wrong with that?

Answer 2: A gives \(0 + 100 = 100\); B gives \(4 + 10 = 14\). The biased estimator wins by a factor of seven, and that is not a trick question — it is this session.
