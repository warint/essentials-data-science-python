What session 4 is for

The mathematics

  • \(X^\top X\) singular when \(p > n\): no unique solution
  • Ridge shrinks by \(1/(1+\lambda)\) in the SVD basis — never to zero
  • The \(\ell_1\) ball has corners, and corners are where zeros come from
  • Soft-thresholding: \(\hat\beta = \mathrm{sign}(z)(|z| - \lambda)_+\)

The Python

  • RidgeCV, LassoCV, ElasticNetCV
  • KFold(shuffle=True), and why the shuffle matters
  • lasso_path for the whole coefficient trajectory
  • StandardScaler — and why a penalty makes it compulsory

The judgement

  • Accept a biased estimator when it lowers total error
  • Never read a lasso’s non-zero set as a list of true effects
  • Notice when two collinear variables carry only one identifiable quantity
  • Say which signals your sample size cannot support

The problem

Sixty bakes. Two hundred ingredients. Seven that matter.

What least squares does here

Xtr, Xte, ytr, yte = train_test_split(Xs, y, test_size=0.35, random_state=7)
ols = LinearRegression().fit(Xtr, ytr)

print(f"train rows {Xtr.shape[0]}, columns {Xtr.shape[1]}")
print(f"training MSE   {mean_squared_error(ytr, ols.predict(Xtr)):.8f}")
print(f"test MSE       {mean_squared_error(yte, ols.predict(Xte)):.2f}")

train rows 39, columns 200
training MSE   0.00000000
test MSE       36.23
variance of y  35.45

Training error is exactly zero. With more columns than rows the model passes through every point, because it has enough freedom to. It has memorised the noise, and on new bakes it does worse than predicting the mean would.

What “no unique solution” means

\[ \hat\beta_{\text{OLS}} = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top y \]

With \(p > n\), \(X^\top X\) is \(200 \times 200\) with rank at most 60. It is singular and cannot be inverted.

There are infinitely many coefficient vectors that fit the training data perfectly. The data cannot choose between them, so we have to add a rule that does. That rule is the penalty, and choosing it is what this session is about.

Bias on purpose

\(\text{Error} = \text{Bias}^2 + \text{Variance} + \sigma^2\)

The trade

\[ \mathbb{E}\left[(y - \hat f(x))^2\right] = \text{Bias}^2 + \text{Variance} + \sigma^2 \]

Least squares is unbiased, which sounds like a virtue. It buys that unbiasedness with variance, and when \(p\) approaches \(n\) the price becomes absurd.

Every estimator in this session is deliberately biased. Each adds a penalty that pulls coefficients towards zero — knowing that is the wrong place for them — because the variance it saves is worth more than the bias it costs.

Standardise first, always

A penalty adds up the coefficients. A coefficient’s size depends on its variable’s units, so an unstandardised penalty punishes whichever variable happens to be measured in small units.

Xs = StandardScaler().fit_transform(X.values)     # mean 0, sd 1, every column
print(f"column means {np.abs(Xs.mean(0)).max():.2e}, sds {Xs.std(0).min():.4f}")
column means 1.48e-15, sds 1.0000

This is not a nicety. Forget it and your penalty is applied in arbitrary units, and the variables it selects are partly an artefact of how somebody chose to record them.

Ridge

Shrink everything, zero nothing

The estimator

\[ \hat\beta_{\text{ridge}} = \arg\min_\beta \; \|y - X\beta\|^2 + \lambda \|\beta\|_2^2 \]

which, unlike OLS, has a closed form that always exists:

\[ \hat\beta_{\text{ridge}} = (X^\top X + \lambda I)^{-1} X^\top y \]

Adding \(\lambda\) to the diagonal makes a singular matrix invertible. Ridge did not merely improve the \(p > n\) problem — it made it solvable.

Why it never reaches zero

In the singular-value basis, ridge multiplies the \(j\)-th component of the OLS solution by

\[ \frac{d_j^2}{d_j^2 + \lambda} \]

  • That factor is strictly between 0 and 1 for any finite \(\lambda\).
  • Directions with little variance (\(d_j\) small) are shrunk hardest — which is exactly where OLS was most unstable.
  • It is never equal to zero, so ridge keeps all 200 coefficients, always.

Ridge on our data

alphas = np.logspace(-2, 4, 25)
ridge = RidgeCV(alphas=alphas, cv=kf).fit(Xs, y)
coef = pd.Series(ridge.coef_, index=names)

print(f"chosen lambda      {ridge.alpha_:.2f}")
print(f"non-zero           {(coef.abs() > 1e-6).sum()} of {len(coef)}")
print(f"above |0.1|        {(coef.abs() > 0.1).sum()}")
print(f"CV MSE             {cv_mse(Ridge(alpha=ridge.alpha_)):.2f}")
chosen lambda      0.01
non-zero           200 of 200
above |0.1|        135
CV MSE             28.50

Two hundred non-zero coefficients, 135 of them large enough to notice. Ridge has not told you which ingredients matter, because it structurally cannot. It gives you a prediction and no list.

Lasso

Where the zeros come from

The estimator

\[ \hat\beta_{\text{lasso}} = \arg\min_\beta \; \|y - X\beta\|^2 + \lambda \|\beta\|_1 \]

One character has changed — \(\ell_2\) to \(\ell_1\) — and the behaviour is qualitatively different.

Corners produce zeros

Both methods minimise the same squared error subject to a budget on \(\beta\). The shape of the budget is what differs.

  • The \(\ell_2\) budget \(\{\|\beta\|_2 \le t\}\) is a circle. A circle is smooth, so the point where the error contours first touch it is generically off both axes: no zeros.
  • The \(\ell_1\) budget \(\{\|\beta\|_1 \le t\}\) is a diamond, with corners on the axes. Contours touching a corner give a solution with a coefficient exactly zero.

Sparsity is a fact about the geometry of the constraint region, not a numerical threshold applied afterwards.

Soft-thresholding, in one variable

With one standardised predictor, the lasso solution is

\[ \hat\beta = \operatorname{sign}(z)\left(|z| - \lambda\right)_+ \]

where \(z\) is the OLS estimate. Everything within \(\lambda\) of zero becomes zero; everything else moves towards zero by exactly \(\lambda\).

Ridge is a rotation towards zero — always proportional, never arriving. The lasso has a flat stretch, and that flat stretch is variable selection.

The lasso on our data

The two leaveners

Two ingredients that are, statistically, one ingredient

What was planted

print(f"corr(baking_soda, baking_powder) = "
      f"{X.baking_soda.corr(X.baking_powder):.4f}")
print(f"true effects: soda {TRUTH['baking_soda']:+.2f}, "
      f"powder {TRUTH['baking_powder']:+.2f}   — identical")
print(f"\nlasso:  soda {lc.baking_soda:+.3f}   powder {lc.baking_powder:+.3f}")
corr(baking_soda, baking_powder) = 0.9632
true effects: soda +1.50, powder +1.50   — identical

lasso:  soda +0.000   powder +1.942

The lasso zeroed one of two ingredients with identical true effects, and gave the survivor most of their combined weight. It does this in every cross-validation split, confidently, and nothing in the output hints that a second ingredient is hiding behind the first.

Why it has no choice

The \(\ell_1\) penalty charges for \(|\beta_1| + |\beta_2|\). Two near-identical columns can deliver the same fit by loading everything onto one of them — which costs the same total but is a corner of the diamond, and corners are where the solution lands. Splitting evenly costs the same and is not a corner.

Ridge does the opposite: the \(\ell_2\) penalty charges for \(\beta_1^2 + \beta_2^2\), and that is minimised by splitting the load. Ridge would share it and select neither.

Elastic net

One penalty with both halves in it

The estimator

\[ \hat\beta = \arg\min_\beta \|y - X\beta\|^2 + \lambda\left(\alpha\|\beta\|_1 + \tfrac{1-\alpha}{2}\|\beta\|_2^2\right) \]

  • \(\alpha = 1\) is the lasso. \(\alpha = 0\) is ridge.
  • The \(\ell_1\) part still produces exact zeros.
  • The \(\ell_2\) part has no reason to prefer either of two identical columns, so it shares between them.

In scikit-learn \(\alpha\) is called l1_ratio. In glmnet it is called alpha, and scikit-learn’s alpha is the overall \(\lambda\). This has caused a great deal of confusion; check which library you are reading.

The grouping effect, measured

enet = ElasticNetCV(l1_ratio=0.95, cv=kf, random_state=0,
                    max_iter=200_000).fit(Xs, y)
ec = pd.Series(enet.coef_, index=names)

pair = ["baking_soda", "baking_powder"]
print(pd.DataFrame({"planted": pd.Series(TRUTH).reindex(pair),
                    "lasso": lc.reindex(pair).round(3),
                    "elastic net": ec.reindex(pair).round(3)}).to_string())
print(f"\npair total  —  lasso {lc[pair].sum():+.3f}   "
      f"elastic net {ec[pair].sum():+.3f}   planted {sum(TRUTH[k] for k in pair):+.3f}")
               planted  lasso  elastic net
baking_soda        1.5  0.000        0.374
baking_powder      1.5  1.942        1.481

pair total  —  lasso +1.942   elastic net +1.855   planted +3.000

The elastic net keeps both. That is the grouping effect, and it is the argument for the method.

But be careful what you claim

for seed in range(5):
    m = ElasticNetCV(l1_ratio=0.95, cv=KFold(10, shuffle=True, random_state=seed),
                     random_state=0, max_iter=200_000).fit(Xs, y)
    c = pd.Series(m.coef_, index=names)
    print(f"CV seed {seed}:  soda {c.baking_soda:+.3f}   powder {c.baking_powder:+.3f}"
          f"   sum {c[pair].sum():+.3f}")
CV seed 0:  soda +0.374   powder +1.481   sum +1.855
CV seed 1:  soda +0.786   powder +1.203   sum +1.989
CV seed 2:  soda +1.318   powder +0.891   sum +2.209
CV seed 3:  soda +0.699   powder +1.248   sum +1.947
CV seed 4:  soda +0.552   powder +1.358   sum +1.910

The sum is stable. The split is not — it moves with an arbitrary choice of cross-validation seed. Two collinear columns carry one identifiable quantity between them, and no penalty conjures a second. The elastic net’s real service is telling you there are two ingredients; it cannot tell you how to divide the credit.

What CV is optimising

Prediction. Not truth.

The lasso selected 25 ingredients. Six are real.

selected            25
genuinely non-zero  7
correctly found     6
false positives     19
missed              ['baking_soda']

This is not a failure. Cross-validation tunes for prediction, and the penalty that predicts best is smaller than the penalty that selects best. Keeping a few extra small coefficients costs almost nothing in error and can help.

A lasso’s non-zero set is not a list of true effects. Reporting it as one — “the model identified 20 drivers of taste” — is the commonest misuse of this method, and it is a misuse of a correct model.

And the signal that got away

weak = "dutch_cocoa"
print(f"{weak}:  planted {TRUTH[weak]:+.2f}")
print(f"            lasso {lc[weak]:+.3f}   elastic net {ec[weak]:+.3f}")
print(f"\nsd of the outcome: {y.std():.2f}")
dutch_cocoa:  planted +1.10
            lasso +0.042   elastic net +0.025

sd of the outcome: 5.95

The weakest planted effect is not recovered by anything, and at 60 observations it should not be — it sits below the detection floor. No choice of method rescues a signal the sample size cannot support, and a method that appears to has found noise.

Three limitations to state out loud

  1. Penalised coefficients are biased, by design. Do not report them as effect sizes without saying so.
  2. The standard errors in the output are not valid after a data-driven selection. There is no honest \(p\)-value on a lasso coefficient without extra machinery — post-selection inference, or a sample split.
  3. Selection is unstable under collinearity. Which of two correlated variables survives is close to arbitrary, and it changes with a resample.

Limitation 2 is not academic. A published table of lasso-selected coefficients with stars beside them is reporting inference that was invalidated by the selection that produced the table.

Key takeaways

  1. When \(p > n\), OLS fits perfectly and predicts terribly. Training error of zero is a symptom, not an achievement.
  2. Every method here is biased on purpose, and that is why it wins.
  3. Standardise before penalising, or the penalty is in arbitrary units.
  4. Ridge shrinks and never zeroes. The lasso zeroes, because the \(\ell_1\) ball has corners.
  5. The lasso must choose between collinear columns; the elastic net shares. But the split is not identified — only the pair’s total.
  6. Cross-validation optimises prediction. Its selected set is not a list of true effects, and its coefficients have no valid \(p\)-values.
