The Bestiary

The only puzzle where you can see the right answer

The brief

1,600 creatures, four species, five measurements. Build a classifier.

This dataset is unusual: it was generated from known distributions, so essentials.bestiary_posterior() returns the true class probabilities at any point. You can compute the best achievable accuracy in advance, and you can draw the true decision boundary underneath your own.

Use that. The point is not to score well — it is to see how your classifier is wrong, which is a thing you almost never get to look at.

Ninety minutes

00–15 Compute the ceiling before fitting anything
15–35 The bias–variance curve train and test, across \(k\)
35–55 Draw your boundary on the truth three values of \(k\)
55–75 The rare species what accuracy concealed
75–85 Three useless columns what they cost, and why
85–90 Write it up 250 words

Stage 1 · Compute the ceiling — 15 min

import essentials as ess
import numpy as np
from sklearn.metrics import accuracy_score

d = ess.load("bestiary")
species = ess.bestiary_species()
post = ess.bestiary_posterior(d.wing_span_cm, d.tail_length_cm)
bayes = np.array(species)[post.argmax(1)]
  1. What is the Bayes accuracy on this sample? Write it at the top of your script.
  2. What is the Bayes balanced accuracy? Why do the two differ?
  3. Why is it not 1.0? Name two distinct reasons from the data-generating process.
  4. If your test accuracy came out at 0.98, what would you conclude?

Stage 2 · The bias–variance curve — 20 min

  1. Split into train and test, stratify=y. Standardise using the training statistics only.
  2. For \(k\) in 1, 3, 5, 9, 15, 25, 45, 75, 125, 250, 400: record training and test accuracy.
  3. Plot both curves against \(k\) on one figure, with the Bayes floor as a horizontal line.
  4. Describe the two curves in one sentence each. They do not have the same shape.

Why stratify=y? Without it, a random split of a 2% class can put almost none of it in the test set — and your evaluation of the class you care about is then based on four creatures.

Stage 3 · Draw your boundary on the truth — 20 min

GX, GT = np.meshgrid(np.linspace(...), np.linspace(...))
truth = ess.bestiary_posterior(GX, GT).argmax(1).reshape(GX.shape)
  1. Build a mesh over the two real features.
  2. For \(k = 1\), your best \(k\), and \(k = 400\): fill the mesh with your predictions and contour the true boundary on top.
  3. Describe each panel. What specifically is \(k = 1\) fitting? What has \(k = 400\) lost?

This is the only time in the course you get to see the answer. Look at it properly — most of what you will ever know about overfitting comes from this picture.

Stage 4 · The rare species — 20 min

  1. Print the confusion matrix at your chosen \(k\). Label it.
  2. Report per-class precision and recall with classification_report.
  3. Now the key measurement: take your predictions, replace every ember_drake prediction with your most common class, and recompute accuracy. How much did total blindness to the rare species cost?
  4. Say in one sentence what that number means for anyone optimising accuracy.
  5. Would you rather have high precision or high recall on ember_drake? Give a scenario for each.

Step 3 is the one that changes how you read every classification result you will ever see.

Stage 5 · Three useless columns — 10 min

mass_g, antenna_mm and scale_count contain no information about species.

  1. Refit with all five features. Report accuracy at several \(k\) against the two-feature version.
  2. Explain the drop in terms of distance, specifically.
  3. Run permutation_importance on the five-feature model. Does it identify the noise?
  4. Why would a penalised regression have coped better?

Also worth trying, if you have time

  • Fit QuadraticDiscriminantAnalysis. It is the correct model family for this generator. Does it beat \(k\)-NN, and by how much?
  • Fit LogisticRegression. A linear boundary on a curved problem — how much does it actually lose? The answer is smaller than you expect, and worth a sentence.
  • Fit an unlimited-depth DecisionTreeClassifier. Which value of \(k\) does its behaviour resemble?

What you hand in

250 words, plus your script:

  • The Bayes floor, and two reasons it is not 1.0
  • Your chosen \(k\), how you chose it, and on which data
  • The train/test curves, described
  • The boundary figure, with the truth overlaid
  • The cost of ignoring the rare species, in accuracy points
  • Whether you want precision or recall on it, and why
  • What the three noise columns cost, and why distance methods suffer

And the standing rule: one output you identified as wrong, and how.

What loses marks

  • Choosing \(k\) on the test set
  • Standardising before splitting (the test statistics leak into training)
  • Reporting accuracy with no confusion matrix and no baseline
  • Reporting accuracy as if it described the rare class
  • “The model is 87% accurate” with no mention of the 86.25% ceiling
  • Using \(k\)-NN on unstandardised features

Two-minute report

One ceiling and why it is not 1.0
One picture your boundary against the true one
One cost what ignoring 2% of the data does to accuracy
