2 · Potential outcomes

For each unit \(i\), imagine two numbers:

\(Y_i(1)\) the outcome if treated
\(Y_i(0)\) the outcome if not treated

The causal effect for that unit is \(Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)\).

You never observe both. A café either has a cat or it does not, so exactly half of every unit’s causal information is missing by construction. This is the fundamental problem of causal inference, and no dataset solves it.

What we estimate instead

We give up on individual effects and estimate an average:

\[ \text{ATT} = \mathbb{E}\left[Y_i(1) - Y_i(0) \mid \text{treated}\right] \]

the average effect on the treated. To get it, we need a stand-in for \(\mathbb{E}[Y_i(0) \mid \text{treated}]\) — the outcome the treated units would have had. That stand-in is what every method in this session is arguing about.

A causal claim is always a claim about a counterfactual, and a counterfactual is never in your data. The design is the argument for the substitute you used.

3 · The 2×2 difference-in-differences

Two groups, two periods. One group gets treated in between.

before after change
treated 50 85 +35
control 35 55 +20
DiD = +15
  • The treated group rose by 35. That is not the effect: things were changing anyway.
  • The control group rose by 20. That is our estimate of what would have happened to the treated group without treatment.
  • The difference of the differences is 15.

The same number, as a regression

\[ Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1\,\text{Treated} + \beta_2\,\text{Post} + \beta_3\,(\text{Treated}\times\text{Post}) + \varepsilon \]

\(\beta_0\) control group, before
\(\beta_1\) how much higher the treated group started
\(\beta_2\) how much everyone changed over time
\(\beta_3\) the DiD estimate

The interaction is the estimate. Work through the table above and confirm that \(\beta_0 = 35\), \(\beta_1 = 15\), \(\beta_2 = 20\), \(\beta_3 = 15\). Do it on paper — it is fifteen minutes that makes the rest of the session readable.

4 · Parallel trends

The assumption doing all the work:

In the absence of treatment, the treated and control groups would have followed the same trend.

  • It does not require the two groups to be similar. Levels can differ freely — \(\beta_1\) absorbs that.
  • It does require their changes to have matched.
  • It is fundamentally untestable, because it is a statement about a counterfactual.

What you can do is check whether trends matched before treatment, and treat that as evidence about the period after. That is the event study, and it is evidence rather than proof.

5 · Load the data

import essentials as ess

d = ess.load("cat_cafe")
d.groupby("cat_arrival_quarter").cafe_id.nunique()

186 cafés observed over 12 quarters. Each adopts a resident cat in quarter 4, 7 or 10. The outcome is average customer dwell time in minutes.

Answer before class:

  1. How many cafés in each adoption cohort? How many are never treated?
  2. Plot mean dwell_min by quarter, one line per cohort. What do you see?
  3. In quarter 11, how many cafés are untreated?
  4. Given your answer to 3 — for a café treated in quarter 10, what would you use as a control group?

Why question 4 matters

There is no good answer, and finding that out before class is the point. By quarter 10 every café has a cat. Nothing is left to serve as a counterfactual, and no estimator can manufacture one.

Keep that in mind on Thursday, because one of the methods we look at will happily report a number for those quarters anyway.

7 · Self-check

  1. Why does difference-in-differences not require the two groups to be similar?
  2. A treated group rose by 12 and a control group rose by 12. What is the DiD estimate, and what does it mean?
  3. Pre-treatment trends are parallel. Does that prove parallel trends holds?
  4. Staggered adoption: units treated in 2018, 2020 and 2022. Which of them could serve as a control for the 2022 cohort, and which could not?

Question 4 is the whole subject of the lecture. If your answer is “the ones not yet treated”, you are ahead of a great deal of published work.

What the session will ask of you

  • Estimate a 2×2 DiD as an interaction, and read every coefficient
  • Explain why two-way fixed effects generalises it, and where that breaks
  • Decompose a two-way fixed effects estimate into its 2×2 comparisons
  • Identify forbidden comparisons — already-treated units used as controls
  • Estimate group-time effects that avoid them
  • Build an event study, and read the pre-treatment periods properly
  • Say how large a violation would have to be to overturn your conclusion
