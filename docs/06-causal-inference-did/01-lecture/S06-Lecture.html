What session 6 is for

The mathematics

  • \(\text{ATT} = \mathbb{E}[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid D = 1]\), and the missing half
  • TWFE is a weighted average of 2×2 comparisons, with some weights negative
  • \(\text{ATT}(g,t)\): one estimate per cohort per period
  • A breakdown value: the violation that would overturn the sign

The Python

  • The interaction in smf.ols is the 2×2 estimate
  • dd.BaconDecomposition — where a TWFE number came from
  • dd.CallawaySantAnna with control_group="not_yet_treated"
  • Building an event study from group-time cells

The judgement

  • Distrust a single treatment dummy under staggered adoption
  • Name your control group for every comparison being made
  • Prefer an estimator that returns NA over one that invents a number
  • Report how large a violation would have to be, not that trends “look parallel”

The estimator everyone reaches for

Unit fixed effects, time fixed effects, one treatment dummy:

\[ Y_{it} = \alpha_i + \gamma_t + \beta\, D_{it} + \varepsilon_{it} \]

twfe = smf.ols("dwell_min ~ has_cat + C(cafe_id) + C(quarter)", data=d).fit()

b = twfe.params["has_cat"]
print(f"has_cat  {b:+.4f}   se {twfe.bse['has_cat']:.4f}   "
      f"t {twfe.tvalues['has_cat']:+.2f}   p {twfe.pvalues['has_cat']:.1e}")
has_cat  -2.0877   se 0.4725   t -4.42   p 1.0e-05

Negative. The cat drove customers away, by two minutes a visit, with a \(t\)-statistic of −4.4 and \(p = 10^{-5}\). Three stars. This is the specification in a large share of published difference-in-differences work.

Every true effect is positive and growing. The estimator returned the wrong sign with a small standard error. Not a noisy zero — a confident, significant, backwards answer.

This is not a contrived failure. It is the central finding of the difference-in-differences literature since about 2018, and the rest of the hour is why it happens and what to do instead.

Decomposing it

Where that number came from

TWFE is a weighted average of 2×2s

Goodman-Bacon (2021) showed that with staggered adoption, a two-way fixed effects estimate is exactly a weighted average of every 2×2 difference-in-differences you could form from the data.

bacon = dd.BaconDecomposition().fit(
    d, outcome="dwell_min", unit="cafe_id", time="quarter",
    first_treat="cat_arrival_quarter")

The arithmetic closes exactly. Half the weight sits on comparisons averaging +8.5; the other half on comparisons averaging −12.6. The two nearly cancel, and what survives is negative.

The forbidden comparisons

Look at the later_vs_earlier rows. In those, the later cohort is the treated group and the earlier cohort is the control group — a control group that already has a cat.

 treated_group  control_group  estimate  weight  time_start  time_end
             7              4  -12.7084  0.2065           4        12
            10              4  -16.7559  0.1999           4        12
            10              7   -3.6033  0.0968           7        12

50.3% of the total weight

Every one of them is negative, and none of them is a treatment effect.

Why they come out negative

Suppose cohort 4’s effect is still growing in quarter 8, and we use cohort 4 as the control group for cohort 7.

  1. Cohort 7’s outcome rises, because its cat just arrived.
  2. Cohort 4’s outcome also rises — because its effect is still growing.
  3. Difference-in-differences subtracts the control’s change from the treated group’s change.
  4. So it subtracts cohort 4’s ongoing treatment effect, and books it as evidence that treatment does nothing — or, if the control’s growth is faster, as evidence of harm.

The control group’s own treatment effect is being subtracted from the treated group’s. That is the whole bug, and one treatment dummy cannot see it.

Two conditions, both needed

  • Staggered adoption. With one common treatment date there are no already-treated controls and TWFE is fine.
  • Effects that change over time or differ across cohorts. With one constant effect everywhere, subtracting the control’s effect subtracts the same number from both sides and it cancels.

Both hold in this data — and both hold in most real policy rollouts, which is why this matters. Dynamic effects are the normal case. Almost nothing works fully on its first day.

The fix

Never use an already-treated unit as a control

Group-time average treatment effects

Callaway and Sant’Anna (2021) refuse to pool. Instead of one \(\beta\), estimate one effect for each cohort \(g\) in each period \(t\):

\[ \text{ATT}(g, t) = \mathbb{E}\left[Y_t(g) - Y_t(0) \mid G = g\right] \]

Each is a clean 2×2, using as controls only units that are not yet treated at time \(t\) — never the already-treated.

cs = dd.CallawaySantAnna(control_group="not_yet_treated").fit(
    d, outcome="dwell_min", unit="cafe_id", time="quarter",
    first_treat="cat_arrival_quarter", aggregate="group")

print(f"overall ATT   {cs.overall_att:+.4f}")
print(f"TWFE          {twfe.params['has_cat']:+.4f}")

How to read the pre-period honestly

pre = cells[(cells.event_time < 0) & cells.est.notna()]
print(f"placebo cells        {len(pre)}")
print(f"mean estimate        {pre.est.mean():+.4f}")
print(f"largest |estimate|   {pre.est.abs().max():.4f}")
print(f"individually significant at 5%: "
      f"{int((pre.est.abs() > 1.96 * pre.se).sum())} of {len(pre)}")
placebo cells        12
mean estimate        -0.0016
largest |estimate|   1.1807
individually significant at 5%: 1 of 12

One of the twelve placebo cells is individually significant. At a 5% level, on twelve tests, you expect about one false rejection — so this is what a design with genuinely parallel trends looks like, not evidence against it.

Pre-trend tests are a multiple-testing problem, and reading them one star at a time will make you reject good designs and, with low power, accept bad ones.

What is not identified

An estimator that returns NA is doing you a favour

Nine cells come back empty

print(missing[["cohort", "quarter", "true_att"]].to_string(index=False))
 cohort  quarter  true_att
      4       10      28.0
      4       11      32.0
      4       12      36.0

      7       11       7.5
      7       12       9.0
     10       10       0.5
     10       11       1.0
     10       12       1.5

Everything from quarter 10 onwards, and cohort 10 entirely. By quarter 10 every café in the sample has a cat, so there is no not-yet-treated unit left. Nothing identifies those effects.

This is the most important slide in the session

  • Callaway–Sant’Anna returns NA for those cells, because the data cannot answer.
  • Two-way fixed effects reported a number for them — with three stars — by quietly using already-treated cafés as controls.

An estimator that refuses is more useful than one that invents. The nine NAs are not a shortcoming of the method; they are a correct description of what this research design can and cannot support.

And it is a lesson about design, not software. If you plan a rollout where everything is treated by the end of your data, you have planned a study that cannot evaluate its own last phase. Keeping a control group untreated is a decision made before any data exists.

How wrong could it be?

Sensitivity, instead of a parallel-trends assertion

Stop testing the assumption. Bound it.

Parallel trends is untestable, so Rambachan and Roth (2023) propose a better question:

How large would a violation have to be before my conclusion changes?

Suppose the treated cohort was on a differential trend of \(\delta\) minutes per quarter. It contaminates \(\text{ATT}(g,t)\) by roughly \(\delta \times (t - g + 1)\). Solve for the \(\delta\) that drives the estimate to zero — the breakdown value.

Computing it

for g, v in cs.group_effects.items():
    n_q = len(cells[(cells.cohort == g) & (cells.event_time >= 0)
                    & cells.est.notna()])
    mean_exposure = np.arange(1, n_q + 1).mean()
    print(f"cohort {g:2d}:  ATT {v['effect']:+7.3f}   "
          f"mean exposure {mean_exposure:.1f} quarters   "
          f"breakdown δ = {v['effect'] / mean_exposure:+.3f} min/quarter")

slope = np.polyfit(pre.event_time, pre.est, 1)[0]
print(f"\nobserved pre-treatment trend slope: {slope:+.4f} min/quarter")
cohort  4:  ATT +13.470   mean exposure 3.5 quarters   breakdown δ = +3.849 min/quarter
cohort  7:  ATT  +3.009   mean exposure 2.0 quarters   breakdown δ = +1.504 min/quarter

observed pre-treatment trend slope: +0.0443 min/quarter

Which gives you a sentence you can defend

“To explain away the cohort-4 effect, the treated cafés would have to have been on a differential trend of 3.85 minutes per quarter. The trend we can measure before adoption is 0.04 minutes per quarter — nearly ninety times smaller. A violation of the required size would have been unmissable in the pre-period.”

That is a far stronger claim than “pre-trends look parallel”, and it is the form the current literature expects. diff_diff implements the full Rambachan–Roth procedure as dd.HonestDiD; the arithmetic above is the same idea, done by hand so you can see what it rests on.

The rest of the toolkit

The same problem produced several estimators. They mostly agree, and knowing they exist matters more than knowing which to prefer:

Callaway & Sant’Anna (2021) group-time ATTs, not-yet-treated controls
Sun & Abraham (2021) interaction-weighted event study
Borusyak, Jaravel & Spiess (2024) impute \(Y(0)\) from untreated observations
de Chaisemartin & D’Haultfœuille (2020) robust to treatment switching off
Gardner (2022) two-stage: remove the fixed effects, then estimate
Arkhangelsky et al. (2021) synthetic DiD — weights units and periods

All of these are in diff_diff. Try two, and report both.

What to report

  • The design first: who is treated, when, and what serves as the control
  • A decomposition if you use TWFE at all, showing the forbidden weight
  • Group-time effects, or a stated reason for pooling
  • An event study, with the pre-period read as a set of tests rather than one
  • What is not identified, explicitly
  • A breakdown value, not an assertion that trends look parallel

A DiD result with no event study, no decomposition and no named control group is an interaction coefficient wearing the word “causal”.

Key takeaways

  1. The interaction in a 2×2 is the DiD estimate. Everything else is that idea under complications.
  2. Under staggered adoption, TWFE is a weighted average of 2×2s — and some weights are negative.
  3. The bug is already-treated units used as controls. When effects grow, the control’s own effect gets subtracted from the treated group’s.
  4. It needs staggered adoption and dynamic or heterogeneous effects. Both are the normal case.
  5. Group-time estimators recover the truth by never making forbidden comparisons.
  6. Read pre-trends as multiple tests, not one star at a time.
  7. An estimator that returns NA is doing you a favour.
  8. Report a breakdown value. “Trends look parallel” is not a robustness check.

Where this leaves you

You now have a toolkit that runs from a single straight line to an estimator that knows the limits of its own identification. The through-line has been the same in all six sessions: the fit statistic is not the diagnostic, and the confident answer is the one to check.

Session 1’s regression fitted at \(R^2 = 0.96\) and reported a meaningless slope. This session’s estimator reported the wrong sign at \(p = 10^{-5}\). In both cases the output looked excellent. In both cases the way to find out was to ask what the method was actually comparing.
